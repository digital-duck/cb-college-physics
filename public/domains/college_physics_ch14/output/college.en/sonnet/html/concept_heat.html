Heat

Heat is the spontaneous transfer of thermal energy between two objects (or between an object and its surroundings) caused by a temperature difference. Energy flows from the hotter object to the colder one until they reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium). Heat is a process quantity: an object does not "contain" heat. It contains internal energy, and heat is the energy in transit. We write $Q$ for heat, measured in joules (J), with $Q>0$ when energy enters the object.

Key relation. For an object that stays in one phase (no melting or boiling),

$$Q = mc\,\Delta T,$$

where $m$ is mass, $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$, and $c$ is the specific heat capacity. This is the energy needed to change 1 kg of the material by 1 K. A material with a large $c$, such as water, changes temperature slowly when energy is added or removed.

Conservation of energy. If no energy escapes, the heat lost by the hot object equals the heat gained by the cold one:

$$\sum Q_i = 0.$$

Worked example

A $0.200\ \text{kg}$ aluminum block at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.500\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\text{C}$. Find the final temperature $T_f$. Use $c_{\text{Al}} = 900\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$ and $c_{\text{w}} = 4186\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$, and assume no energy escapes.

Both objects end at $T_f$, and $Q_{\text{Al}} + Q_{\text{w}} = 0$:

$$(0.200)(900)(T_f - 100) + (0.500)(4186)(T_f - 20) = 0$$ $$180\,T_f - 18000 + 2093\,T_f - 41860 = 0$$ $$2273\,T_f = 59860 \;\Rightarrow\; T_f \approx 26.3\,^\circ\text{C}.$$

The result is close to the water's starting temperature. The water's $mc = 2093$ J/K is far larger than the metal's $180$ J/K, so the water's temperature changes much less.

Problem-solving application

Problem. A $0.150\ \text{kg}$ sample of an unknown metal is heated to $95.0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and dropped into $0.400\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20.0\,^\circ\text{C}$. The system settles at $23.0\,^\circ\text{C}$. Identify the metal's specific heat capacity, assuming no energy escapes.

Strategy. Compute the heat the water gains, which is fully known. Then use conservation of energy to find the unknown $c$.

  • Water gains: $Q_{\text{w}} = (0.400)(4186)(23.0 - 20.0) = 5023\ \text{J}$.
  • The metal must lose the same amount: $Q_{\text{m}} = -5023\ \text{J}$.
  • Write the metal's heat in terms of $c$:
    • $$Q_{\text{m}} = (0.150)\,c\,(23.0 - 95.0) = -10.8\,c.$$
  • Solve:
    • $$-10.8\,c = -5023 \;\Rightarrow\; c \approx 465\ \text{J/(kg·K)}.$$

    This is close to the value for iron ($\approx 450$ J/(kg·K)), so iron or steel is a likely candidate. The difference could come from small heat losses to the container.

    Checklist for heat problems:

  • List every object with its mass, specific heat, and initial temperature.
  • Write $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ for each object, always using $T_f - T_i$.
  • Apply $\sum Q = 0$ for an isolated system and solve for the unknown.
  • Check the signs. The hotter object must have $Q<0$ and the colder object $Q>0$. The final temperature must lie between the two starting temperatures.
