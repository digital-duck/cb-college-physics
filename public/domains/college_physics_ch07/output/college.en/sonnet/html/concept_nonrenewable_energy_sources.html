Nonrenewable Energy Sources

Nonrenewable energy sources—coal, petroleum, and natural gas—are fossil fuels formed from the remains of organisms buried and transformed by heat and pressure over millions of years. Because the geological processes that create them operate on timescales of $10^6$ to $10^8$ years, while human extraction operates on timescales of decades, these resources are effectively fixed stocks: once consumed, they are not replenished on any timescale relevant to civilization. Combustion releases the chemical energy stored in carbon-hydrogen bonds, producing carbon dioxide and water as byproducts, and this same reaction is what drives the buildup of atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ responsible for anthropogenic climate forcing.

The key quantitative tool for reasoning about a finite resource is the reserves-to-production (R/P) ratio, defined as

$$ R/P = \frac{\text{proved reserves (barrels, tons, or } m^3\text{)}}{\text{annual production rate}}. $$

This gives a naive "years remaining" estimate assuming constant extraction. But consumption rarely stays constant—it typically grows, which changes the depletion math substantially. If consumption grows at a constant fractional rate $r$ per year starting from current rate $P_0$, the resource with total reserves $R$ is exhausted at time $T$ satisfying

$$ R = \int_0^T P_0 e^{rt}\,dt = \frac{P_0}{r}\left(e^{rT}-1\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad T = \frac{1}{r}\ln\!\left(\frac{rR}{P_0}+1\right). $$

Worked example: Suppose a country has coal reserves $R = 4000$ million tons, current annual production $P_0 = 100$ million tons/year, and demand grows at $r = 0.03$ (3% per year). The naive static R/P ratio gives $4000/100 = 40$ years. Using the exponential-growth formula instead:

$$ T = \frac{1}{0.03}\ln\!\left(\frac{0.03 \times 4000}{100}+1\right) = \frac{1}{0.03}\ln(2.2) \approx 26.4 \text{ years}. $$

Growth in demand shortens the effective lifetime by nearly 14 years compared to the static estimate—an important lesson: linear "years remaining" figures reported in the media routinely overstate resource longevity when consumption is rising.

The fossil fuel life cycle: slow geological formation feeds a comparatively rapid extraction-combustion cycle, coupling energy release to carbon emissions.

Problem-solving application: Given a country's proved reserves, current production, and an assumed growth rate, the exponential depletion formula lets policymakers and analysts estimate a more realistic resource horizon than the static R/P ratio, informing decisions about import dependency, substitution timelines for renewables, and carbon-budget planning.
