Concept Book: Magnifying Glass

College Physics Ch25 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A magnifying glass is a single converging lens used with the object inside its focal length. It forms an upright, enlarged virtual image. This is the natural endpoint of the text because it uses every earlier idea at once: refraction at curved surfaces, the thin-lens equation, image formation, and the role of the eye as the final optical element.

What it achieves. The lens does not enlarge the object itself. It lets you bring the object closer than the eye's near point, about $25\ \text{cm}$, while the eye still sees a sharp image. The angular magnification, with the image at infinity (a relaxed eye), is

$$M = \frac{25\ \text{cm}}{f}.$$

Worked example. A lens with $f = 5\ \text{cm}$ gives $M = 5$. To check the image, place an object at $d_o = 4\ \text{cm}$. The thin-lens equation $\frac{1}{d_o}+\frac{1}{d_i}=\frac{1}{f}$ gives

$$\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{5}-\frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1}{20}, \quad d_i = -20\ \text{cm}.$$

The negative sign means a virtual image on the same side as the object. The lateral magnification is $m = -d_i/d_o = 5$, and the image is upright. Because $d_o < f$, the rays diverge after the lens, so no real image forms on a screen. Your eye traces the rays back to the virtual image.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you need $M = 10$ to read fine print. Then $f = 25/10 = 2.5\ \text{cm}$. This design has a limit. A shorter focal length needs a more strongly curved lens, which brings more aberration. It also forces a smaller working distance. Practical design is therefore a trade-off among $M$, field of view, and image quality.

Connections to applications. The same few equations apply across optical instruments:

  • Microscopes: an objective lens makes a real, enlarged intermediate image. The eyepiece is a magnifying glass that views it, so $M_{\text{total}} \approx m_{\text{obj}}\cdot M_{\text{eye}}$.
  • Telescopes: the eyepiece again acts as a magnifier. It examines the real image formed by a long-focal-length objective, giving $M = f_{\text{obj}}/f_{\text{eye}}$.
  • Corrective eyewear and reading aids: a converging lens supplies the extra focusing power a presbyopic eye lacks, and the thin-lens equation sets the prescription in diopters, $P = 1/f$.
  • Cameras and sensors: the same imaging relations decide where to place the sensor. They also show why a close object needs more lens extension.

    • An invitation. Choose one application, such as a compound microscope, and design it. Select $f_{\text{obj}}$ and $f_{\text{eye}}$, set the tube length, and compute the total magnification. Then ask what limits it. You will find that diffraction, not arithmetic, finally caps useful magnification.
