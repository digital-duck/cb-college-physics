Thermal Stress

Definition

When a material is heated, it expands; when cooled, it contracts. If it is free to move, no stress develops. Thermal stress arises when that expansion or contraction is constrained, for example by rigid walls, clamps, or neighboring material at a different temperature. The constraint forces the material to deform mechanically by the amount it would otherwise have changed thermally, and that mechanical deformation is stress.

For a temperature change $\Delta T$, the free (unconstrained) fractional length change is

$$\frac{\Delta L}{L} = \alpha\,\Delta T,$$

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of linear thermal expansion. If the ends are fixed so the net length change is zero, the mechanical strain must cancel the thermal strain, $\varepsilon = -\alpha\,\Delta T$. With Young's modulus $E$ (Hooke's law, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$):

$$\sigma = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T.$$

The negative sign means heating a constrained rod produces compressive stress; cooling produces tensile stress.

Volume version. For a material confined on all sides, the volumetric expansion is $\Delta V/V = \beta\,\Delta T$, with $\beta \approx 3\alpha$ for isotropic solids. The bulk modulus $B$ relates pressure change to fractional volume change, $\Delta p = -B\,\Delta V/V$. If the volume is held fixed, the confined material must be compressed by $-\beta\Delta T$, so

$$\Delta p = B\,\beta\,\Delta T.$$

Worked example

A steel rail ($E = 200\ \text{GPa}$, $\alpha = 12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) is laid tightly between fixed anchors at $10^\circ\text{C}$. On a summer day it reaches $40^\circ\text{C}$. Find the stress.

$$\sigma = -E\alpha\,\Delta T = -(200\times10^{9})(12\times10^{-6})(30) = -7.2\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}$$

The rail carries about $72$ MPa of compression. Since steel's yield strength is a few hundred MPa, this is survivable, but it helps explain why long rails buckle sideways on hot days.

Problem-solving application

Sealed liquid. A rigid container is completely filled with water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ and sealed. Water has $\beta \approx 2.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ and $B \approx 2.2\ \text{GPa}$. The temperature rises by $10\ \text{K}$. Estimate the pressure rise.

Ignoring the container's own expansion,

$$\Delta p = B\beta\,\Delta T = (2.2\times10^{9})(2.1\times10^{-4})(10) \approx 4.6\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa} \approx 46\ \text{atm}.$$

A modest 10 K warming produces a pressure that can rupture pipes or tanks, which is why sealed hydraulic systems include expansion tanks or relief valves.

General strategy:

  • Decide whether the constraint is one-dimensional (use $E$, $\alpha$) or all-around (use $B$, $\beta$).
  • Compute the free thermal strain, $\alpha\Delta T$ or $\beta\Delta T$.
  • Require the total strain to match the constraint (usually zero); the mechanical strain is the negative of the thermal strain.
  • Multiply by the appropriate modulus and compare with the material's yield or fracture limit.

    • For partial constraint, such as a rod between slightly flexible supports or a gap of width $g$, subtract the allowed movement first: $\varepsilon = -(\alpha\Delta T - g/L)$. Engineers use expansion joints in bridges and gaps in railway track for exactly this reason: allowing the free thermal strain removes the stress.
