Linear Thermal Expansion

Definition. When a solid is heated, its atoms vibrate with larger amplitude and, because the interatomic force is asymmetric, their average spacing increases. For a small temperature change, the resulting change in length is proportional to both the original length and the temperature change:

$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \,\Delta T$$

Here $L_0$ is the original length, $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$ is the temperature change, and $\alpha$ is the coefficient of linear expansion. Its units are $\text{K}^{-1}$ (equivalently $^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), and its value depends on the material. Typical values are about $12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for steel, $23\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for aluminum, and $3\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for Pyrex glass. The final length is $L = L_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)$. If $\Delta T<0$, the object contracts. The linear relation holds well only when $\alpha\Delta T \ll 1$, which is true for ordinary temperature ranges.

Worked example. A steel bridge girder is $L_0 = 40.0\ \text{m}$ long at $-10^\circ\text{C}$. How much longer is it at $35^\circ\text{C}$?

$$\Delta T = 35-(-10) = 45\ \text{K}$$ $$\Delta L = (12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})(40.0\ \text{m})(45\ \text{K}) = 2.16\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} \approx 2.2\ \text{cm}$$

The girder grows by about 2 cm, which is only 0.05% of its length. That is small, but it is large enough to buckle a rigid structure.

Problem-solving application: constrained expansion. Engineers leave expansion gaps in bridges and railway tracks. Suppose a steel rail of length $L_0$ is clamped so it cannot expand. The thermal strain it would have had is

$$\frac{\Delta L}{L_0} = \alpha\,\Delta T,$$

and the wall must compress the rail by that same fractional amount. Using Young's modulus $Y$, the stress is

$$\frac{F}{A} = Y\,\alpha\,\Delta T.$$

For steel, $Y \approx 2.0\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}$. With $\Delta T = 45\ \text{K}$:

$$\frac{F}{A} = (2.0\times10^{11})(12\times10^{-6})(45) \approx 1.1\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa}.$$

That is about 108 MPa, a large fraction of steel's yield strength. This is why rails buckle on hot days if they are not pre-stressed or given gaps.

Strategy for any problem:

  • Identify $L_0$ and compute $\Delta T$ with its sign.
  • Look up $\alpha$ for the material.
  • Compute $\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T$, or the final length $L=L_0+\Delta L$.
  • If the object is constrained, convert the blocked expansion into stress with $Y\alpha\Delta T$.
  • Check the answer. $\Delta L$ should be tiny compared with $L_0$.

    • Related case: two materials. When two materials with different $\alpha$ are bonded, as in a bimetallic strip, heating makes the higher-$\alpha$ side lengthen more, so the strip bends toward the lower-$\alpha$ side. This is the principle behind mechanical thermostats.
