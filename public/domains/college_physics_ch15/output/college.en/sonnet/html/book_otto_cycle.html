Concept Book: Otto Cycle

College Physics Ch15 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The Otto cycle is where everything you have learned about gases, energy, and entropy comes together in one working machine: the idealized model of a spark-ignition engine. It achieves something specific. It lets you take a few measurable inputs (a compression ratio, a gas, an intake state, a heat input) and predict the thermal efficiency and net work of a real engine, with no hand-waving.

What it achieves. The cycle has four reversible steps: isentropic compression ($1\to2$), constant-volume heat addition ($2\to3$), isentropic expansion ($3\to4$), and constant-volume heat rejection ($4\to1$). For an ideal gas with constant specific heats, the thermal efficiency reduces to a single expression:

$$\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,\gamma-1}}, \qquad r = \frac{V_1}{V_2}$$

Here $r$ is the compression ratio and $\gamma = c_p/c_v$. The derivation uses $Q_{\text{in}} = mc_v(T_3-T_2)$, $Q_{\text{out}} = mc_v(T_4-T_1)$, and the isentropic relation $T V^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}$. For air ($\gamma = 1.4$) and $r = 9$, $\eta = 1 - 9^{-0.4} \approx 0.585$. Real engines reach roughly 25–35%, and the gap shows how much friction, heat loss, and finite combustion time cost.

Why it is the natural endpoint. The cycle needs every earlier concept. The first law gives the work and heat balances. The ideal gas law and $\gamma$ give the state relations. Isentropic processes give the temperature–volume links. The second law explains why $Q_{\text{out}}$ can never be zero. It also leaves you with a design variable, $r$, that you can optimize.

Connections to applications. The application list for this section was empty, so these are the domains where the cycle most often gets used.

  • Automotive design: The formula explains why engineers push compression ratios up. It also shows why they stop, since high $r$ causes knock, which is premature ignition of the fuel–air mixture.
  • Fuel selection: Octane rating sets how large $r$ can safely be, so fuel and engine are chosen together.
  • Comparing cycles: Placing the Otto cycle beside the Diesel and Brayton cycles on the same $P$–$V$ and $T$–$s$ diagrams lets you rank designs by efficiency and by what limits each one.
  • Energy policy and emissions: A few points of efficiency, multiplied across a billion vehicles, mean large changes in fuel use and CO$_2$.

    • Problem-solving check. Suppose a design raises $r$ from 8 to 10 with $\gamma = 1.4$. The efficiency goes from $1-8^{-0.4}\approx 0.565$ to $1-10^{-0.4}\approx 0.602$. That is a 3.7-point gain from one geometric change. Notice that the gain per unit of $r$ shrinks as $r$ grows. This diminishing return is the kind of conclusion the model lets you reach by calculation.

    Invitation. Choose one application and take it further. A good first project is to model a real engine. Look up its compression ratio and peak temperature, compute the ideal Otto efficiency, then compare that with its published efficiency. Account for the difference term by term, and you will have moved from learning the cycle to using it.
