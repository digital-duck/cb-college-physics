Concept Book: Musical Instrument Resonance

College Physics Ch17 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every concept in this book has been building toward one physical object: a vibrating string or air column that rings at exactly the frequencies it is built to favor. Musical instrument resonance is the natural endpoint because it combines the three ideas we developed separately: oscillation, boundary conditions, and driven response.

What resonance achieves

Consider a string of length $L$ fixed at both ends, with tension $T$ and linear mass density $\mu$. The wave speed is $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$. The boundary conditions allow only standing waves with

$$f_n = \frac{n v}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

The instrument does not create energy. It selects which frequencies the energy can occupy, and the body or air column then amplifies and radiates them. A driven oscillator with natural frequency $f_0$ and damping $\gamma$ responds with amplitude that peaks sharply near $f_0$. The sharpness is measured by the quality factor $Q = f_0/\Delta f$, where $\Delta f$ is the width of the peak at half power. High $Q$ gives a clear, sustained tone. Low $Q$ gives a short, broad thud.

Worked example. A guitar string has $L = 0.65\ \text{m}$, $T = 70\ \text{N}$, and $\mu = 0.0004\ \text{kg/m}$. Then $v=\sqrt{70/0.0004}\approx 418\ \text{m/s}$, so $f_1 = 418/1.3 \approx 322\ \text{Hz}$. The harmonics are near 644, 966, and 1288 Hz. Shortening the string to $0.5\ \text{m}$ by pressing a fret gives $f_1\approx 418\ \text{Hz}$, a rise of about 30%. This is why fret spacing follows a geometric pattern.

Why it is the endpoint

Resonance is where the physics produces something we perceive. The relative strengths of the harmonics, set by where the string is plucked and how the body responds, determine timbre. Two instruments playing the same $f_1$ sound different because their harmonic amplitudes differ.

Connections to applications

The same mathematics appears wherever a structure has natural frequencies:

  • Acoustic design. Concert halls and instrument bodies are tuned so resonances reinforce desired frequencies without ringing at unwanted ones. Designers adjust $L$, $T$, and geometry using the relations above.
  • Engineering safety. A bridge or building has its own $f_n$. If periodic forcing, such as wind or marching, matches one of them, the response grows large. Damping, which lowers $Q$, is the standard remedy.
  • Medical imaging and ultrasound. Resonant transducers emit and detect narrow frequency bands, and magnetic resonance imaging relies on matching a drive frequency to a nuclear resonance.
  • Signal processing and synthesis. Digital filters and physical-modeling synthesizers reproduce a resonance by choosing $f_0$ and $Q$ as parameters.
  • Electronics. An $LC$ circuit resonates at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, the principle behind radio tuning.

    • An invitation

    Pick one application and test it yourself. Build a one-string instrument from fishing line and a weight, and measure $f_1$ against your prediction as you change $L$. Or record a note and compute its spectrum to see the harmonic amplitudes. Either project will make the formula $f_n = nv/2L$ something you have heard and verified, not just derived.
