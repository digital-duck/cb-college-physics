Concept Book: Barometer

College Physics Ch11 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A barometer measures one thing, atmospheric pressure, and from that one number it lets you infer many others. That is why it is the natural endpoint of this book. Every earlier idea (fluid statics, pressure as force per area, the weight of a column of fluid) comes together in a single instrument you can build and read.

What the barometer achieves. In a mercury barometer, a tube sealed at the top and inverted in a dish of mercury holds a column of height $h$. Above the column is a near-vacuum, so the pressure at the mercury surface in the dish, which is atmospheric pressure, must balance the weight of the column per unit area:

$$P_{\text{atm}} = \rho g h$$

Worked check. With $\rho = 13{,}600\ \text{kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, and $h = 0.760\ \text{m}$:

$$P_{\text{atm}} = (13{,}600)(9.81)(0.760) \approx 1.01\times10^5\ \text{Pa}$$

This is standard sea-level pressure. The result also explains why mercury is used. Water is about 13.6 times less dense, so its column would be about 10.3 m tall, which is impractical to carry. The instrument therefore turns an invisible quantity into a length you can read with a ruler.

Connection to applications.

Weather forecasting. Falling pressure usually signals an approaching low-pressure system, with clouds and precipitation. Rising pressure signals clearing, stable air. A drop of 3 mmHg over three hours is a meaningful warning, and a reading that small is easy to see on a mercury column.

Altimetry. Pressure decreases with height because there is less air overhead. For modest altitudes, $\Delta P \approx -\rho_{\text{air}}\, g\, \Delta z$. Near sea level, $\rho_{\text{air}} \approx 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$, so a 100 m climb lowers the pressure by about $(1.2)(9.81)(100)\approx 1.2\ \text{kPa}$. This is the principle behind aircraft altimeters, hiking watches, and the barometric sensors in phones. Because the air's density also falls with height, a more accurate model uses the exponential form $P = P_0 e^{-z/H}$, with scale height $H\approx 8$ km.

Vacuum and gauge measurement. Many pressure instruments report gauge pressure, which is measured relative to the atmosphere. Absolute pressure is $P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{gauge}} + P_{\text{atm}}$. A barometer supplies the $P_{\text{atm}}$ needed for that conversion. Without it, you cannot convert a tire gauge reading or a vacuum-chamber reading into absolute terms.

Boiling point and cooking. Water boils when its vapor pressure equals the surrounding pressure. At lower $P_{\text{atm}}$, as in mountain towns, water boils below $100^\circ\text{C}$, which is why cooking times change.

Problem to try. A hiker's barometer reads 700 mmHg at camp, and it read 760 mmHg at the trailhead. Estimate the altitude gain using the linear model, converting the pressure difference to pascals first.

An invitation. Each of these applications is a full subject in itself. If one caught your interest, pick it and go deeper. Altimetry is a good starting point. Collect pressure readings at several known elevations, fit them to $P=P_0e^{-z/H}$, and see how closely your own data recovers the scale height. You will have used the barometer as a working scientific instrument and not only as a formula.
