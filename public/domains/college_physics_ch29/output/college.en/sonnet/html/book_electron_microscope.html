What the electron microscope achieves. A light microscope cannot resolve features much smaller than about half the wavelength of visible light, roughly 200 nm. This is the Abbe diffraction limit:

$$d \approx \frac{\lambda}{2\,\mathrm{NA}}$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength and $\mathrm{NA}$ is the numerical aperture of the lens. To see smaller structures you need a smaller $\lambda$. Matter waves provide one. The de Broglie relation gives an electron's wavelength as $\lambda = h/p$. For an electron accelerated through a potential $V$, the non-relativistic result is

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e e V}}.$$

Worked example. Take $V = 100\ \text{kV}$. Using $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$, $m_e = 9.11\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$ and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$:

$$\lambda = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.602\times10^{-19})(1\times10^{5})}} \approx 3.9\times10^{-12}\ \text{m}.$$

At this voltage electrons move at a large fraction of light speed, so a relativistic correction is needed. It gives about 3.7 pm. Either way, the wavelength is about 100,000 times shorter than that of green light. Real instruments fall far short of this limit because lens aberrations dominate, but they still resolve individual atoms.

Why it is the natural endpoint. The microscope draws on several earlier ideas at once:

  • wave–particle duality
  • electromagnetic forces that steer charged particles
  • potential-energy conversion, which sets the electron's speed
  • diffraction, which sets the resolution limit

    • Each idea has to work correctly for the instrument to form an image.

    Problem-solving application. The same reasoning lets you size up any imaging problem. Given a feature size $d$ you want to resolve, estimate the $\lambda$ you need, then solve for the accelerating voltage. For $d = 0.1\ \text{nm}$, requiring $\lambda \lesssim 0.01\ \text{nm}$ gives $V \gtrsim 15\ \text{kV}$ in the non-relativistic formula. That is well within ordinary instruments.
