Concept Book: Millikan Oil Drop Experiment

College Physics Ch30 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Millikan's oil drop experiment is the natural endpoint of this book because it turns every earlier idea into one measurement. It combines electrostatics, fluid drag, and statistical reasoning, and it ends with a result that changes how we see matter: electric charge comes in discrete units.

What it achieves. A charged oil droplet falls through air. At terminal velocity $v_f$, gravity (less buoyancy) balances Stokes drag:

$$mg = 6\pi\eta r v_f.$$

Apply a vertical field $E = V/d$ and the droplet rises at speed $v_r$. Now the electric force balances gravity plus drag:

$$qE = mg + 6\pi\eta r v_r.$$

Eliminating the mass using $m = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\Delta\rho$ gives the radius from the fall speed, $r = \sqrt{9\eta v_f / (2\Delta\rho\, g)}$. Then the charge follows:

$$q = \frac{6\pi\eta r\,(v_f+v_r)}{E}.$$

Worked example. Suppose $\eta = 1.8\times10^{-5}\ \text{Pa·s}$, $\Delta\rho = 860\ \text{kg/m}^3$, and $v_f = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}$. Then $r \approx 1.1\times10^{-6}$ m. With $E = 3.0\times10^{5}$ V/m and $v_r = 1.3\times10^{-4}$ m/s, we get $q \approx 3.2\times10^{-19}$ C, which is $2e$.

Problem-solving application. One droplet proves little. Repeat the measurement on many droplets, then look for the largest $e$ that divides every charge: $q_i = n_i e$ with integer $n_i$. A droplet that picks up an extra electron under X-rays changes $n_i$ by exactly one, so $\Delta q = e$ directly. The data cluster at $1.602\times10^{-19}$ C and its integer multiples. This is the result: charge is quantized. Measurement error matters too. Because $q \propto r$ and $r \propto \sqrt{v_f}$, a 2% timing error in $v_f$ gives about a 1% error in $q$. Slip correction for very small droplets is an important systematic refinement.

Why it is the endpoint. Each earlier tool, such as force balance, drag laws, uniform fields, and error propagation, becomes a step in one argument. The experiment also models good science: a controlled setup, a measurable quantity, and an integer pattern in noisy data.

Applications.

  • Atomic and particle physics. A known $e$ lets J. J. Thomson's ratio $e/m$ yield the electron mass, and it anchors the charge assignments of quarks ($\pm\tfrac13 e$, $\pm\tfrac23 e$).
  • Metrology. Since 2019 the SI defines the ampere by fixing $e$ exactly. Counting elementary charges is now the foundation of electrical units.
  • Electronics and instrumentation. Single-electron devices, charge-coupled sensors, and electrostatic precipitators depend on discrete charge and on the same force-balance reasoning.
  • Aerosol science. Air quality monitors and cleanroom particle counters size particles by their drag and electric mobility, which is the oil-drop method applied in practice.
