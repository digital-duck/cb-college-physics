Concept Book: Calorimetry

College Physics Ch14 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Calorimetry is the measurement of heat. Every calorimeter, from a foam coffee cup to a bomb calorimeter, applies one idea: energy is conserved, so heat lost by one part of an isolated system must equal heat gained by another. We write this as

$$\sum_i Q_i = 0, \qquad Q = mc\,\Delta T \quad\text{(no phase change)}.$$

We can't observe heat directly. A thermometer reads temperature, and the specific heat $c$ links that reading to energy. Calorimetry is the natural endpoint of this book because it uses every earlier idea together: temperature, heat capacity, phase changes, conservation of energy, and the difference between a system and its surroundings. It turns them into one method for finding unknown quantities.

Worked example. A 0.200 kg metal sample at $100.0\,^\circ\text{C}$ goes into 0.500 kg of water at $20.0\,^\circ\text{C}$, and the mixture settles at $22.4\,^\circ\text{C}$. With $c_w = 4186\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$, the metal's specific heat is

$$c_m = \frac{m_w c_w (T_f - T_w)}{m_m (T_m - T_f)} = \frac{(0.500)(4186)(2.4)}{(0.200)(77.6)} \approx 324\ \text{J/(kg·K)}.$$

That value is close to copper's, so the sample is probably copper. Nothing in the setup identified the material. We found it by balancing energy.

Applications.

  • Materials science and engineering. The same balance gives specific heats, latent heats, and heats of reaction. Engineers use these values to choose heat sinks, engine materials, and insulation. Differential scanning calorimetry detects melting and phase transitions in polymers and alloys.
  • Chemistry. A bomb calorimeter holds a reaction at constant volume, so $Q_{\text{reaction}} = -C_{\text{cal}}\,\Delta T$, where $C_{\text{cal}}$ is the calorimeter's heat capacity. This gives enthalpies of combustion, which are the basis of thermochemical tables.
  • Nutrition and biology. Food Calories were originally measured by burning food in a calorimeter. Metabolic researchers still measure an organism's heat output to find its energy use.
  • Energy and environmental science. Fuel heating values, thermal storage, and the heat budget of a building or lake all rest on the same accounting.

    • Problem-solving habit. Draw the system boundary, list every object that exchanges heat, and write one $Q$ per object. Then set the sum to zero and solve. If ice is present, add $mL$ terms for any phase change. Real calorimeters lose some heat, so a careful experimenter checks the result against the cup's own heat capacity.

    Where to go next. Pick one domain and take it all the way through. You could measure the enthalpy of dissolving a salt with a foam-cup calorimeter. You could estimate the Calories in a snack by burning it under a water-filled can. Or you could compare the thermal behavior of two materials. Whichever you choose, write the energy balance first, predict the temperature change, and then test the prediction. The gap between prediction and measurement is where the real science starts.
