Concept Book: Cathode Ray Tube

College Physics Ch22 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The cathode ray tube (CRT) is the natural endpoint of this book because it forces every earlier idea to work at once. A CRT is a sealed, evacuated glass envelope in which a heated cathode emits electrons, an electric field accelerates them, and electric or magnetic fields steer them onto a phosphor screen. Each step uses a law you have already learned.

What the CRT achieves. Start with energy conservation. An electron (charge $e$, mass $m$) accelerated through a potential difference $V$ gains kinetic energy $eV$, so its speed is

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m}}.$$

Worked example. For $V = 2000\ \text{V}$, $e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, and $m = 9.11\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.60\times10^{-19})(2000)}{9.11\times10^{-31}}} \approx 2.65\times10^{7}\ \text{m/s},$$

about 9% of the speed of light. The nonrelativistic formula is still a good approximation at this speed.

Next, steering. In a uniform deflection field $E$ across plates of length $L$, the electron's transverse acceleration is $a = eE/m$. It crosses the plates in time $t = L/v$, so it leaves with transverse velocity $v_y = at$. The beam is displaced on a screen at distance $D$ by

$$y \approx \frac{eEL}{mv^{2}}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}+D\right) = \frac{EL}{2V}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}+D\right).$$

The mass and charge cancel. The deflection is linear in the control field $E$ and falls as the accelerating voltage $V$ rises. That linearity is what makes the CRT a precise instrument and not just a curiosity.

Why it is the endpoint. Kinematics, electrostatics, magnetism, and energy methods all appear in one device. Solving a CRT problem means choosing the right law for each stage and chaining the results.

Connections to applications. No application domains were specified for this concept. The CRT's main uses can still be traced to the formulas above.

  • Oscilloscopes. The deflection formula makes $y$ proportional to the input voltage. A sawtooth voltage on the horizontal plates sweeps time across the screen, so the trace is a graph of signal against time.
  • Television and monitors. Magnetic coils steer the beam in a raster scan. Beam current sets brightness at each point.
  • Particle physics. J. J. Thomson used crossed $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ fields in a CRT to measure $e/m$. Balancing $eE = evB$ gives $v = E/B$. Removing $E$ leaves a circular path of radius $r = mv/(eB)$, which yields $e/m$.

    • Invitation. Choose one application and work it through. A good start is the oscilloscope. Design a sweep that displays a 1 kHz sine wave over exactly two periods, and calculate the plate voltage needed for a 4 cm deflection with your choice of $V$, $L$, and $D$. Or reproduce Thomson's measurement from a set of field values. In either case, you will be using the same few laws you have spent this book learning.
