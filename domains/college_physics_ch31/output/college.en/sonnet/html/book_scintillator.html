Concept Book: Scintillator

College Physics Ch31 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A scintillator is a material that converts the energy of a single ionizing particle or photon into a short burst of visible or near-visible light. It is the natural endpoint of this book because it joins several ideas we have built separately: energy deposition, atomic excitation, statistical fluctuation, and signal detection. Following one gamma ray through a scintillator uses all of them.

What a scintillator achieves. A particle depositing energy $E$ excites electrons across the material's band gap. When they de-excite, a fraction $\eta$ of the energy (the scintillation efficiency) leaves as photons of mean energy $\bar{E}_\gamma$. The mean photon yield is

$$N_\gamma = \frac{\eta E}{\bar{E}_\gamma}.$$

The light output is therefore proportional to the deposited energy. That proportionality is what makes the scintillator a spectrometer and not just a trigger.

Worked example. Sodium iodide doped with thallium, NaI(Tl), produces about 38 photons per keV. A 662 keV gamma ray from $^{137}$Cs, absorbed fully, gives

$$N_\gamma \approx 38 \times 662 \approx 2.5\times 10^{4}\ \text{photons}.$$

Each photon is a random event, so $N_\gamma$ fluctuates by Poisson statistics with standard deviation $\sqrt{N_\gamma}\approx 159$. The relative spread is $1/\sqrt{N_\gamma}\approx 0.6\%$. This is the statistical floor on energy resolution. Real detectors do worse, typically around 7% at 662 keV, because light is lost in collection and in the readout.

Connection to the photomultiplier. About $2.5\times10^4$ photons carry far too little energy to measure directly. A photomultiplier tube (PMT) closes this gap. A photocathode with quantum efficiency $Q\approx 0.25$ converts photons to photoelectrons, giving about $6\times10^{3}$ electrons. Those electrons then pass through $n$ dynodes, each with secondary-emission gain $\delta$, so the total gain is

$$G=\delta^{\,n}.$$

With $\delta=4$ and $n=10$, $G\approx 10^{6}$. The pulse then holds about $6\times10^{9}$ electrons, or roughly $10^{-9}$ C, which an ordinary amplifier can read. The pulse height is proportional to $E$, so a histogram of pulse heights is a gamma-ray energy spectrum.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you need a detector with 3% resolution at 662 keV, and the Poisson floor is the only limit. You need $1/\sqrt{N_{pe}}\le 0.03$, which gives $N_{pe}\ge 1111$ photoelectrons. At $Q=0.25$ that requires at least 4444 collected photons, or about $E\ge 117$ keV for NaI(Tl). The design question becomes how much of that light you can collect and convert.

An invitation. The scintillator–PMT pair appears in PET scanners, in dark-matter searches, in neutrino observatories, and in radiation monitoring at airports. Choose one and rebuild its resolution budget the way we just did. Start with the photon yield, subtract the losses, and apply the gain. The same few equations will tell you what the instrument can and cannot see.
