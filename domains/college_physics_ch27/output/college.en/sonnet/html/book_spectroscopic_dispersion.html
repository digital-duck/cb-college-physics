Concept Book: Spectroscopic Dispersion

College Physics Ch27 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The concept of spectroscopic dispersion ends this book for a simple reason. It turns a single beam of mixed light into a measurable function, so every earlier idea (waves, refraction, interference, diffraction) now has a job to do.

What it achieves. Dispersion is the dependence of a wave's propagation on its wavelength. In a prism, the refractive index $n(\lambda)$ decreases as $\lambda$ increases (normal dispersion). The deviation angle therefore depends on color. A diffraction grating with spacing $d$ separates wavelengths by a different mechanism, constructive interference:

$$d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda$$

The angular dispersion is

$$D = \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d\cos\theta_m}$$

Both devices map wavelength to position. Once that map is calibrated, a detector can read position as wavelength and intensity as amount. The result is a spectrum $I(\lambda)$.

Worked example. A grating has 600 lines/mm, so $d = 1.67\ \mu\text{m}$. Find the first-order angle for $\lambda = 589\ \text{nm}$:

$$\sin\theta_1 = \frac{589\times10^{-9}}{1.67\times10^{-6}} \approx 0.353 \Rightarrow \theta_1 \approx 20.7^\circ$$

For $\lambda = 589.6\ \text{nm}$ (the other sodium line), $D \approx 6.0\times10^{5}\ \text{rad/m}$, so the lines are separated by about $4\times10^{-7}\ \text{rad}$. The resolving power $R = \lambda/\Delta\lambda = mN$ shows how many illuminated lines $N$ you need. Here $R \approx 980$, so $N \gtrsim 980$ lines (about 1.6 mm of grating) are enough to resolve the sodium doublet.

Why it is the natural endpoint. Each earlier topic supplies a piece of the instrument. Refraction and interference supply the wavelength-to-angle map. Diffraction sets the resolution limit. Photon energy $E = hc/\lambda$ links each measured line to a quantum transition. A spectrum therefore ties wave optics to atomic structure.

Applications. This section lists no specific application domains. The same logic carries into any setting where light carries information. In astronomy, redshifted lines give velocity through $\Delta\lambda/\lambda = v/c$. In chemistry and environmental monitoring, absorption lines identify molecules and their concentrations. In optical communications, wavelength separation lets many channels share one fiber. In materials and medical analysis, emission or absorption signatures reveal composition without contact.

Problem to try. Your detector has pixels 10 μm wide and a focusing lens of focal length 0.5 m. Using $D$ from above, how wide a wavelength band does one pixel cover in first order at $\theta_1 = 20.7^\circ$? Does it resolve the sodium doublet?

Invitation. Choose one application and build its pipeline end to end. Pick a source, a dispersive element, a detector and a calibration. Then ask what limits your measurement: resolution, noise, or the physics of the sample itself. I suggest starting with stellar spectra. A single measured shift in wavelength there tells you how fast a distant star is moving.
