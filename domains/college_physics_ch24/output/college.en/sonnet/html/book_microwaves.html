Concept Book: Microwaves

College Physics Ch24 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every idea in this book has been building toward one claim: light, radio waves, and heat are the same thing. Maxwell's equations describe electromagnetic fields in any medium. Microwaves, with frequencies from roughly $300\ \text{MHz}$ to $300\ \text{GHz}$ and wavelengths from $1\ \text{m}$ down to $1\ \text{mm}$, are where that theory becomes a working technology. Their wavelengths are comparable to the size of everyday objects like cavities, antennas and aircraft. Their fields are also strong enough to push on matter. That is why they are the natural endpoint.

What microwaves achieve. Two facts from earlier chapters carry the whole story. First, waves obey $c = f\lambda$, so at $f = 10\ \text{GHz}$ the wavelength is

$$\lambda = \frac{3\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}{10^{10}\ \text{Hz}} = 3\ \text{cm}.$$

A wave this short can be focused into a narrow beam by a dish of modest size and reflected by objects of human scale. Second, a wave entering a material loses energy according to the material's complex permittivity $\varepsilon = \varepsilon' - j\varepsilon''$, where $\varepsilon''$ measures how strongly the material absorbs energy from the field. Microwaves are short enough to be directed and are strongly absorbed by many materials. This gives two applications that look different but rest on the same physics.

Radar. A radar transmitter sends a pulse and times the echo. Because the pulse travels at $c$ and covers the distance twice, the target range is

$$R = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}.$$

Worked example: an echo returns after $\Delta t = 200\ \mu\text{s}$, so $R = (3\times10^{8})(2\times10^{-4})/2 = 30\ \text{km}$. The Doppler shift of the returning wave gives speed: for a target moving toward the radar at speed $v \ll c$, $\Delta f \approx 2vf/c$. At $f = 10\ \text{GHz}$ and $v = 30\ \text{m/s}$, $\Delta f = 2(30)(10^{10})/(3\times10^{8}) = 2\ \text{kHz}$. Reflection, wavelength, beam focusing and the Doppler effect all appear in this one device.

Dielectric heating. Here the absorption term $\varepsilon''$ does the work. Polar molecules such as water try to align with the oscillating field, and friction in that motion turns field energy into heat. The power absorbed per unit volume is

$$P = 2\pi f\,\varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon''\,E^{2}.$$

Problem-solving use: a household oven runs at $f = 2.45\ \text{GHz}$. Doubling the field amplitude $E$ quadruples $P$, and a material with larger $\varepsilon''$ heats faster. This is why food heats while dry ceramic stays cool. The same relation guides industrial drying, plastic welding and medical hyperthermia.

Radar uses the reflected wave, and dielectric heating uses the absorbed wave. Both are consequences of the same boundary conditions and material response you have already learned.

Where to go next. Choose one application and work it through in full. For radar, design a system by choosing $f$, pulse length and antenna size to detect a target at a required range. For dielectric heating, estimate how long an oven takes to heat a cup of water by $40^\circ\text{C}$, given $P$ and the specific heat of water. Either problem uses every tool in this book.
