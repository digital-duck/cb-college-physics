Concept Book: Gravitational Wave Detection

College Physics Ch34 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What gravitational-wave detection achieves. A gravitational wave is a ripple in spacetime that changes the distance between free masses by a tiny fraction, the strain $h = \Delta L / L$. For a merging black-hole pair, $h \sim 10^{-21}$. Detecting it means measuring a length change of about $10^{-18}$ m on a 4 km interferometer arm. That is roughly a thousandth of a proton's diameter.

This is the natural endpoint of the book because it needs every earlier idea at once.

  • General relativity predicts the signal, through the linearized wave equation $\Box \bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}/c^4$.
  • Wave optics and interference turn strain into a measurable light signal.
  • Fourier analysis and statistics pull a faint signal out of noise.

    • Worked example: why the signal is detectable at all. Take a Michelson interferometer with arm length $L = 4\ \text{km}$ and strain $h = 10^{-21}$. The arm-length change is

    $$\Delta L = hL = (10^{-21})(4\times10^{3}\ \text{m}) = 4\times10^{-18}\ \text{m}.$$

    The two arms respond oppositely, one stretching while the other shrinks, so the differential change is $\Delta L_1 - \Delta L_2 = hL$. Light of wavelength $\lambda = 1064$ nm shifts in phase by $\Delta\phi = 4\pi\,\Delta L/\lambda \approx 5\times10^{-11}$ rad. Laser power, Fabry–Perot cavities that fold the light path many times, and power recycling raise the effective sensitivity. The signal then rises above shot noise.

    Problem-solving application: matched filtering. The noisy data is $d(t) = n(t) + h(t)$. Compare it with a template $\tilde h(f)$ from relativity. The signal-to-noise ratio is

    $$\rho^2 = 4\int_0^\infty \frac{|\tilde h(f)|^2}{S_n(f)}\,df,$$

    where $S_n(f)$ is the noise power spectral density. Because the integrand weights each frequency by $1/S_n$, the filter listens where the detector is quiet. A detection claim requires $\rho \gtrsim 8$ in a single detector, confirmed by coincidence with a second. The template's chirp rate also gives the chirp mass,

    $$\mathcal{M} = \frac{(m_1 m_2)^{3/5}}{(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}},$$

    so measuring $\dot f$ and $f$ gives $\mathcal M$ directly. The inverse problem is treated this way: from a waveform, infer the source.

    Connections to applications. This section lists no formal application domains. The same method still extends to several areas:

  • Astrophysics: Measuring $\mathcal M$ and the spins gives black-hole and neutron-star populations that light cannot show.
  • Cosmology: A merger's waveform gives its luminosity distance directly. Pairing it with a redshift from an optical counterpart yields the Hubble constant independently of the distance ladder.
  • Fundamental physics: Comparing observed waveforms with general relativity's predictions tests the theory in the strong-field regime.
  • Signal processing and instrumentation: Matched filtering, noise modeling, and quantum-limited optics carry over to radar, medical imaging, and sensing.

    • An invitation. Choose one application and take it further. A good start is the cosmology route. Use the chirp-mass relation to estimate the distance to a merger from its amplitude, then ask how large the uncertainty in $H_0$ would be after 50 such events. That exercise uses the whole book and ends with a number you can compare against the published measurements.
