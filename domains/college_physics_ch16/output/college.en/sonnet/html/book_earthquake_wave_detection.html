Concept Book: Earthquake Wave Detection

College Physics Ch16 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every idea in this book has been building toward one task: looking at a noisy ground-motion record and deciding, quickly and defensibly, when an earthquake wave arrives and what kind it is. Earthquake wave detection is the natural endpoint because it demands every earlier tool at once. These tools are signals, filtering, windowing, statistics, and decision thresholds.

What it achieves

A seismometer records ground velocity $x(t)$. Most of the time it records background noise. When an earthquake occurs, the fast P-wave (about $6\ \text{km/s}$) arrives first. The slower, stronger S-wave (about $3.5\ \text{km/s}$) follows. Detection means finding the P-wave onset time $t_P$ against noise.

The classic detector is the STA/LTA ratio. It compares the average signal energy in a short window to the average in a long window:

$$R(t) = \frac{\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i=0}^{N_s-1} x^2(t-i\,\Delta t)}{\frac{1}{N_l}\sum_{i=0}^{N_l-1} x^2(t-i\,\Delta t)}, \qquad N_s \ll N_l.$$

In quiet conditions $R \approx 1$. When a wave arrives, the short window responds almost immediately and the long window barely moves, so $R$ jumps. A trigger is declared when $R(t) > R_{\text{on}}$, often around 3 to 5.

Worked example

Suppose the long-term average power is $4$ (in arbitrary units) and a wave pushes the short-term average to $22$. Then $R = 22/4 = 5.5 > 4$, so the detector fires. Using the delay between P and S arrivals, $\Delta t_{S-P}$, the distance to the source follows from

$$d = \Delta t_{S-P}\left(\frac{1}{v_S}-\frac{1}{v_P}\right)^{-1}.$$

With $\Delta t_{S-P}=10\ \text{s}$, $v_P=6$ and $v_S=3.5\ \text{km/s}$, this gives $d \approx 84\ \text{km}$. Stations at three or more locations then fix the epicenter by intersecting distance circles.

Connections to applications

Earlier chapters supplied each piece, and each reappears in a new setting:

  • Early warning systems: P-wave detection gives seconds of notice before the damaging S-wave arrives. Here the choice of $R_{\text{on}}$ is a trade-off between missed events and false alarms.
  • Structural monitoring: The same energy-ratio logic flags abnormal vibration in bridges and buildings.
  • Industrial and medical signals: Machine-fault detection and heartbeat or seizure detection use the same short-window-versus-baseline comparison, with different frequency bands.
  • Event discrimination: Filtering and spectral analysis help separate earthquakes from explosions, traffic, or storms.

    • An invitation

    Pick one application and build it. Download a public seismogram, implement $R(t)$, and tune $N_s$, $N_l$, and $R_{\text{on}}$ until you catch the P-wave without false triggers. Or take a week of accelerometer data from a phone on a bridge rail and ask what "abnormal" means there. Either way, you will find that detection is not just computation. It is a judgment about uncertainty, and that judgment is what you now know how to make.
